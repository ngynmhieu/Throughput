Queueing concepts for Throughput

Each concept below stands on its own. Read them in any order.

Every concept has the same three parts:
  1. What it is: the idea in plain words.
  2. Diagram: one simple picture of how it works.
  3. In Throughput: how it could become something the player does or sees.

1 · Arrivals and speed

1Arrival waves core

What it is

Passengers don't arrive evenly. They come in waves tied to flight times. A checkpoint can cope with the average and still collapse during a wave.

In Throughput

Each level shows a flight board during Plan. The waves are the level's puzzle: the player designs staffing to survive the peaks, not the average.

2Service variability core

What it is

Two lanes can have the same average speed but behave very differently. A lane where service times swing wildly (some 10 s, some 3 min) builds much longer queues than a steady one.

In Throughput

Passenger types have different speeds and spreads (business traveller = fast and steady, family = slow and unpredictable). A "standardize" option, such as a bin pre-loading area, makes service more even without making it faster on average.

3Utilization (the "hockey stick") core

What it is

Utilization = how busy staff are (arrivals ÷ what the staff can handle). Waits stay small for a long time, then explode as staff get close to 100% busy. At 100% or more, the queue never stops growing.

In Throughput

Each stage shows a busy gauge (green / yellow / red). The cheapest-looking plan, with everyone at 98% busy, fails badly. Adding just one staff member to a red stage can cut the wait from 25 minutes to 6. This is the main "feel" of the whole game.

2 · Layout

4Pooling: one shared line beats separate lines core

What it is

With separate lines, one officer can sit idle while another line is long. With one shared line, the next passenger always goes to whichever officer is free, so nobody is idle while people wait.

In Throughput

The player decides this by placing barriers: a serpentine shared line or separate lines per lane. Early lesson: same staff, same passengers, roughly half the wait.

5Dedicated lanes can win (ownership) twist

What it is

The exception to #4. A real hospital study (Song, Tucker & Murrell, 2015) found that when doctors had their own patient queue, they worked faster: stays were about 17% shorter. People take ownership of "their" line. Pure math ignores that.

In Throughput

A late-game "team ownership" staff trait: dedicated lanes get a speed bonus. It surprises players who learned in #4 that shared lines always win.

6Bottleneck core

What it is

When passengers pass through several stages in a row, the whole checkpoint can only go as fast as its slowest stage. Adding staff anywhere else changes nothing.

In Throughput

Checkpoints have multiple stages. After a run, the slowest stage glows red. Big "aha": "I added three officers at ID check and nothing happened."

7Blocking (limited space between stages) core

What it is

If the waiting area between two stages is small and fills up, the stage before it must stop. It has passengers to serve but nowhere to send them. The problem is downstream, but it shows up upstream.

In Throughput

Floor space becomes a resource. Barriers and waiting areas take space, and making an area too small freezes the stage before it. Technical note: the simulation must track space from day one if we want this.

8Shifting bottleneck core

What it is

Fix the slowest stage and a different stage becomes the slowest. Improvement is a chain: fix, watch, find the next one.

In Throughput

This suits Plan → Watch perfectly. Levels are designed so the first obvious fix exposes a second problem, which gives the player a reason to edit and run again.

3 · Who goes next

9Convoy effect core

What it is

In first-come-first-served order, one very slow passenger at the front holds up many quick ones behind them. A single slow job inflates everyone's wait.

In Throughput

Very easy to see during Watch: one passenger freezes a whole lane. It sets up the problem that the express lane (#10) solves.

10Express lane (shortest job first) core

What it is

Serving quick passengers first gives the lowest average wait. In practice: a separate lane for quick passengers, so they never get stuck behind slow ones.

In Throughput

A lane rule set in Plan: "this lane accepts only ≤1 bag". It fits the "every fix has a cost" principle.

11Priority vs fairness core

What it is

Giving one group priority makes them faster, but the work doesn't disappear. Everyone else gets slower. Priority moves waiting around rather than removing it.

In Throughput

A priority lane toggle. The result screen shows the worst-off group, not just the average, so the player sees who paid for the speed-up.

4 · Which lane

12Routing rules core

What it is

When a passenger arrives, which lane should they join? Random is bad. "Shortest line" is good. "Least work" (shortest line, counting slow passengers as heavier) is best when passengers vary.

In Throughput

A routing rule at the entrance, chosen in Plan: Random / Shortest line / Least work / By passenger type.

13Power of two choices twist

What it is

A surprising maths result: if each passenger checks just 2 random lanes and joins the shorter one, you get almost the same benefit as checking every lane (Mitzenmacher). Peeking at two is nearly as good as knowing everything.

In Throughput

Two routing upgrades: a cheap "guide" officer (looks at 2 lanes) vs an expensive live display board (shows all lanes). Players discover the cheap one is almost as good: "half the price, 90% of the effect."

14Closing a lane can help (Braess paradox) signature

What it is

When passengers choose for themselves, each picks what looks fastest for them. Adding a new shortcut can make everyone crowd the same places, so everyone gets slower. Removing the shortcut fixes it (Cohen & Kelly, 1990).

In Throughput

A signature level: a two-checkpoint airport where the winning move is to close something. Needs a decision first: this only works if passengers pick their own route. Right now the player sets all routing.

15Lane switching (jockeying) twist

What it is

Passengers already in a line jump to another line that looks shorter.

In Throughput

Mostly flavour for Watch: it makes passengers feel alive. It acts like an automatic, imperfect "shortest line" rule. Leave it for later, since it makes the math harder to check.

5 · Passenger behaviour

16Balking (refusing to join) twist

What it is

A passenger sees a long line and never joins. They go to another checkpoint or complain.

In Throughput

Each passenger has a "too long" limit based on the line they see. Wait-time signs change this: an honest "≈5 min" sign can stop people leaving a line that only looks long.

17Reneging (giving up mid-wait) core

What it is

A passenger joins, waits, and leaves when their patience runs out. At an airport, patience depends on how soon their flight leaves.

In Throughput

A patience bar over each passenger. Running out = missed flight, which is the main failure count for a run. Pairs naturally with a "tight connection" priority rule (#11).

6 · Staffing rules

18Open/close rules with a gap (hysteresis) core

What it is

A rule like "open lane 4 when the queue is over 20". If the close rule is almost the same ("close when under 19"), the lane flickers open and shut constantly. A gap between the two numbers ("close when under 5") makes it stable.

In Throughput

Since the player can't act during Watch, these rules are how they handle peaks. Each lane gets "open when…" and "close when…" settings during Plan. This is the heart of the rule system.

19Setup time core

What it is

Opening a lane isn't instant. Staff walk over and set up, maybe 2 minutes. So a rule must fire before the wave hits, not when it's already bad.

In Throughput

A visible "opening…" timer on the lane. Without it, the best rule is just "open at 1" and there's no puzzle. With it, the player must anticipate the wave, and planning matters.

20Breaks and shift changes twist

What it is

Staff can't work forever. Officers leave for breaks, which temporarily reduces capacity.

In Throughput

A level twist: the player schedules breaks around the flight board during Plan.

7 · Demand and feeling

21Booked slots (virtual queue, like Disney FastPass) twist

What it is

Give some passengers a booked time to come through security. Their arrivals become evenly spaced instead of clumped, which reduces waiting. But Disney found a side effect: the normal (standby) line got much slower, because booked people still use the same officers.

In Throughput

A late-game option: "offer booked slots to X% of passengers." It helps bookers and hurts standby passengers, a clear trade-off for the player to balance.

22Perceived wait (how long it feels) candidate

What it is

From David Maister's The Psychology of Waiting Lines (1985): the same wait can feel short or long. Waits feel longer when they're uncertain, unexplained, unfair, empty (nothing to do), or happen before the service has even started.

In Throughput

Passenger happiness follows the felt wait, not only the real one. Cheap tools change it: wait-time signs (less uncertainty), screens (time feels filled), no visible queue-jumping (fairness), a bin pre-load area (the wait feels like it has started). Some levels could be won without a faster queue, just one that feels better.

8 · Hidden math tools (for you, not the player)

23Little's Law hidden tool

What it is

People in line = arrival rate × time each person waits. It works for almost any queue. Flip it around to estimate the wait from what you can see: wait ≈ people in line ÷ how many get through per minute.

In Throughput

Powers the "≈ 6 min" wait-time signs over lanes and the numbers on the results screen.

24Kingman's formula hidden tool

What it is

A quick estimate of the wait: Wait ≈ Variability × Utilization × Service time. The parts multiply, so messy, unpredictable service hurts most when staff are already very busy. It combines #2 and #3 into one number.

In Throughput

Gives the "Expected wait ≈ X min" preview before the player presses Run. It also helps you tune levels without running the full simulation.

25Erlang C and Erlang A hidden tool

What it is

Textbook formulas for "how long will people wait with N officers?". Erlang C assumes nobody gives up; Erlang A includes people who leave. A side result: twice the passengers needs less than twice the staff (bigger systems are more efficient).

In Throughput

Your correctness test: run the simulation and check it matches the formula. If it does, you can trust the game's numbers.
